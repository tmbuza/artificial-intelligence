AI Hackathon: Choose the Problem and Plan

Published

Sep 2026

  • ID: AI-L12
  • Type: Guided hackathon, stage 1 of 3
  • Focus: Choose one feasible user task and plan a testable AI prototype
  • Output: A problem brief, source and permission plan, baseline, case set, and build plan

What the hackathon means here

A hackathon is a focused period for making and testing a small solution. In this guide, it is a structured learning project that you can complete alone or with a team. The outcome is a working prototype and an honest account of its evidence, limitations, and next steps. Speed helps you focus; it does not justify using unapproved data or skipping evaluation.

Chapters 12–14 form a sequence. This chapter selects the problem and prepares the materials. Chapter 13 builds and tests one bounded workflow. Chapter 14 presents the results and decides what to improve. The same steps work over a single day, a weekend, or several shorter sessions; choose a schedule that gives you enough time to review outputs carefully.

Pick a task, not a technology demonstration

Start with a real user and one repeatable unit of work. The continuing example is a reviewer who prepares a four-field summary of an approved research article. The prototype may draft the study aim, study design, main finding, and stated limitations, with passage references. A reviewer checks every field before using it.

The first version does not need to process an entire literature collection, interpret every figure, publish a report, or make a research decision automatically. It needs to answer one useful question: Does an evidence-linked draft help the reviewer complete this defined summary reliably and with acceptable effort?

A candidate task is suitable for the hackathon if:

  • a specific person can describe how they do it now;
  • permitted example inputs are available;
  • the expected output can be reviewed;
  • a simple baseline can be completed in the available time;
  • failure cases can be named in advance; and
  • the scope is small enough to build and evaluate honestly.

If the task depends on inaccessible documents, uncertain permission, or an unreviewable outcome, narrow it or choose another task.

Write the problem brief

Use this template:

For [user], given [permitted input], the prototype will produce [defined output] to support [specific task]. A person will review [items requiring judgment]. We will compare it with [baseline] using [representative cases and measures]. The first version will not handle [explicit boundary].

For the article example:

For a research reviewer, given one approved article with readable text, the prototype will draft four summary fields with passage references. The reviewer will verify every field against the article. We will compare it with a manually completed template on reserved articles, measuring claim support, missing-field behavior, and total reviewer time. The first version will flag unreadable scans and figures for manual review.

The brief is short because its purpose is to guide decisions during the build. Save it with a version or date and revise it openly if evidence changes the scope.

Define the baseline and success criteria

The baseline is the current or simplest credible way to complete the same task: a reviewer reading the article and filling the four-field template, possibly using ordinary text search. Record what “complete” means for both workflows. A faster first draft is not necessarily a faster reviewed summary.

Agree on criteria before seeing the model’s best examples. For the article task, criteria could include:

Dimension Question Evidence to collect
Support Does each substantive field match its cited passage? Reviewer judgments and source locations
Missing information Does the draft abstain when the supplied evidence lacks a field? Reserved absence cases
Coverage Are all four fields addressed or explicitly marked unsupported? Output validation
Reviewer effort Does the complete review save useful time? Time for baseline and assisted workflow
Critical errors Does a draft reverse or overstate a result? Severity-labeled corrections

Set thresholds appropriate to the intended use and review capacity. A pilot can produce evidence for revising thresholds, but do not move them after results solely to claim success.

Select and protect the case set

Gather a small set of approved examples that reflect the intended work. Include straightforward and difficult cases. Before generating any model output, identify where the answer is supported or why a field should be marked unsupported.

Separate cases by purpose:

  • Development cases help refine extraction, prompts, and formatting.
  • Reserved evaluation cases test the chosen version after development.

Once a reserved case is used to tune the workflow, label it a development case and replace it for the final comparison. A handful of cases can demonstrate failures and guide learning, but cannot justify broad claims about reliability.

For the article example, begin with a clear text article, one with qualified findings, one with an absent field, and one with unusual section headings. Add scanned or visual cases only if the first version is intended to handle them and you can check them properly.

Decide which parts need AI

Map the workflow before selecting a model:

Step First approach When AI might help
Choose article Manual selection and fixed ID Usually unnecessary
Extract text Existing document extraction A visual or OCR component for scans if needed
Find evidence Section lookup or keyword search Semantic ranking for varied wording
Draft fields Manual template Language-model draft from approved passages
Check format Ordinary program validation Usually unnecessary
Verify meaning Human review against source Model assistance cannot replace the agreed review

The plan may use an existing model, a simple rule, both, or no generative AI for a particular step. Choose what the comparison can test. Do not spend the hackathon training a large model when the learning question concerns evidence-linked drafting.

Set data and action boundaries

Before the build, record which article materials can be used and where they may go. Identify whether the service is hosted or local, whether prompts and responses are logged, and whether any source material is confidential or licensed. For the first run, use material you have permission to process.

Limit tools to the approved sources and necessary operations. A prototype that reads an article and writes a local draft needs less authority than one that changes shared records or sends messages. Define reviewer approval before any external publication or consequential use. Keep credentials out of files, screenshots, and example output.

Plan the smallest working version

A practical first version can consist of:

  1. One approved, readable article with stable passage IDs.
  2. A manual or simple-search baseline.
  3. A prompt and four-field output contract from Chapter 07.
  4. A bounded way to supply relevant article text.
  5. A check for required fields and resolvable source IDs.
  6. A reviewer worksheet and error log from Chapters 09–11.

Write a “done for the build” definition: the workflow runs on at least one development case, produces a provisional draft, detects malformed output, and gives the reviewer the original passages needed to verify claims. Evaluation on reserved cases follows in Chapter 13.

Roles and schedule

If working in a team, assign responsibility for article permission and reference answers, implementation, evaluation, and the final presentation. One person may fill multiple roles, but the reviewer should not silently accept outputs from the person who tuned the prompt without applying the agreed source checks.

A simple schedule is:

Stage Main activity Checkpoint
Plan Scope, sources, baseline, cases Approved problem brief and evaluation criteria
Build Intake, retrieval, prompt, validation End-to-end provisional draft
Test Reserved cases and reviewer checks Error log and baseline comparison
Present Evidence, limits, next decision Demo and decision record

Adjust time allocations to your available period. Protect time for testing; a prototype without an evaluated output has not answered the hackathon question.

Practice: complete the hackathon brief

Create a one-page brief with the following headings:

  1. User and task.
  2. Permitted input and intended output.
  3. In-scope and out-of-scope cases.
  4. Baseline and measurable success criteria.
  5. Development and reserved cases, with reference answers.
  6. Proposed approach and source trace.
  7. Data permissions, tool boundaries, and human approval.
  8. Team roles or individual checklist.
  9. Build checkpoint and final presentation deliverables.

Read the brief as if you were a reviewer unfamiliar with the idea. If they cannot tell what a correct output looks like or what would count as failure, narrow and clarify the task before building.
